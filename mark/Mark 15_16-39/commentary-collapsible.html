Mark 15:16-39

The Crucified King Who Opens the Way

OrientationBig Picture: What's Happening Here? ⌄

Mark brings us to Golgotha without softening the scene. Jesus is mocked by soldiers, dressed in royal costume as a joke, crowned with thorns, struck, spit on, and led out to be crucified. The title over him says, "the king of the Jews" (v. 26, NIV), but everyone treats his kingship as absurd. Soldiers mock him. Passersby insult him. Religious leaders sneer. Even those crucified with him heap insults on him.

The mockers keep circling around one demand: save yourself. Come down from the cross. Prove who you are by escaping weakness. But Mark wants us to see the terrible glory of the cross: Jesus saves others by not saving himself. His kingship is not revealed by avoiding shame, violence, abandonment, and death. It is revealed by giving himself through them.

Then darkness covers the land, Jesus cries out from Psalm 22, and he dies with a loud cry. At that moment, the temple curtain is torn in two from top to bottom, and a Gentile centurion says, "Surely this man was the Son of God!" (v. 39, NIV). The Gospel that began by announcing Jesus as the Son of God now brings that confession to the foot of the cross. The crucified one is the Son. His death opens the way to God.

Mark 15:16-20 | Soldiers Mock Jesus As King

The soldiers lead Jesus into the palace, call together the whole company, and dress him in purple (vv. 16-17). They twist together a crown of thorns and set it on him. Then they begin to call out, "Hail, king of the Jews!" (v. 18, NIV). Everything is staged as mock enthronement.

The irony is unbearable and beautiful. They are pretending Jesus is king in order to humiliate him. But Mark has been telling us from the beginning that Jesus truly is the King. The soldiers mock what they do not understand.

They strike him on the head with a staff, spit on him, and fall on their knees in fake homage (v. 19). This is not harmless ridicule. It is bodily violence joined to dehumanizing contempt. Jesus' kingship is being publicly shamed by the power that will execute him.

After mocking him, they take off the purple robe, put his own clothes back on him, and lead him out to crucify him (v. 20). The true King is led to his throne, but the throne is a cross.

Mark 15:21-27 | Jesus Is Crucified At Golgotha

A man named Simon of Cyrene, the father of Alexander and Rufus, is forced to carry Jesus' cross (v. 21). Jesus has told his disciples to take up their cross and follow him, and now another man is compelled to carry the wood behind the condemned King. Mark does not pause to tell us Simon's thoughts. He simply places him in the path of Jesus' suffering.

They bring Jesus to Golgotha, which means "the place of the skull" (v. 22, NIV). They offer him wine mixed with myrrh, but he does not take it (v. 23). Then Mark says it with dreadful simplicity: "And they crucified him" (v. 24, NIV).

The soldiers divide up his clothes by casting lots to see what each would get (v. 24). While Jesus hangs exposed, others gamble over what is left of him. The shame is total. His body is nailed up. His garments are taken. His dignity is mocked.

It is nine in the morning when they crucify him (v. 25). The written notice of the charge against him reads, "the king of the Jews" (v. 26, NIV). Again the irony is sharp. Rome means the sign as accusation and warning. Mark lets it stand as truth. Jesus is King, but his kingship is being revealed in a way no one expected.

They crucify two rebels with him, one on his right and one on his left (v. 27). Earlier James and John had asked to sit at Jesus' right and left in glory. Now the places at his right and left are occupied by crucified rebels. Mark's Gospel has been teaching us that glory and the cross belong together, and here the lesson is written in blood.

Mark 15:28-32 | The Crucified King Is Mocked To Save Himself

Those who pass by hurl insults at Jesus, shaking their heads and saying, "So! You who are going to destroy the temple and build it in three days, come down from the cross and save yourself!" (vv. 29-30, NIV). They twist the temple accusation into mockery. They assume that if Jesus had power, he would use it to escape suffering.

The chief priests and teachers of the law mock him among themselves: "He saved others," they say, "but he can't save himself!" (v. 31, NIV). They mean it as proof that he is powerless. But their mockery unknowingly tells the truth. If Jesus is to save others, he will not save himself. His refusal to come down is not failure. It is love.

They say, "Let this Messiah, this king of Israel, come down now from the cross, that we may see and believe" (v. 32, NIV). They demand sight before faith, spectacle before trust, escape before allegiance. But if Jesus came down, he would not be doing the saving work he came to do.

Even those crucified with him heap insults on him (v. 32). Jesus is surrounded by contempt from every side: soldiers, passersby, leaders, and fellow sufferers. The loneliness deepens. He is not admired as noble. He is despised as weak.

This scene exposes our instincts too. We often want a Jesus who proves himself by avoiding the cross, not one who saves by enduring it. We want power that comes down, not love that stays.

Mark 15:33-37 | Darkness Falls And Jesus Dies

At noon, darkness comes over the whole land until three in the afternoon (v. 33). The brightest part of the day becomes dark. Creation itself seems to bear witness that this death is no ordinary death. Judgment, grief, and mystery cover the scene.

At three, Jesus cries out in a loud voice, "Eloi, Eloi, lema sabachthani?" which means, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (v. 34, NIV). Jesus is praying Psalm 22. This is not polished religious speech. It is lament from the depths.

We should not explain this cry away too quickly. Jesus enters real abandonment. He cries to God as "my God," but the cry is still forsakenness. The Son who heard the Father's delight at his baptism now hangs in darkness and cries out from the place of judgment.

Some misunderstand and think he is calling Elijah (v. 35). Someone runs, fills a sponge with wine vinegar, puts it on a staff, and offers it to Jesus to drink, saying, "Now leave him alone. Let's see if Elijah comes to take him down" (v. 36, NIV). Even at the edge of death, the watching continues as spectacle.

Then Jesus gives a loud cry and breathes his last (v. 37). Mark does not let us rush past that sentence. Jesus dies. The Son of God truly enters death. The cross is not theater. It is not merely a symbol. The body of Jesus stops breathing.

Mark 15:38-39 | The Curtain Tears And The Centurion Confesses

At the moment of Jesus' death, the curtain of the temple is torn in two from top to bottom (v. 38). This is God's action. The tearing is from above. The barrier associated with the holy presence of God is opened as Jesus dies.

Mark does not explain every layer of temple symbolism, but the meaning is strong enough to feel: access has been opened. The way into God's presence is not opened by human achievement, religious performance, or temple control. It is opened through the death of Jesus.

Then the centurion, standing in front of Jesus, sees how he dies and says, "Surely this man was the Son of God!" (v. 39, NIV). A Roman soldier, part of the machinery of execution, becomes the first human voice in Mark to confess Jesus this way after the cross. The confession comes not after a miracle of escape, but after Jesus' death.

This is Mark's strange and holy revelation. The Son of God is known at the cross. The King is revealed in shame. The way to God is opened through the torn flesh of the crucified one. The mockers wanted Jesus to come down so they could believe. The centurion sees him die and confesses.

SynthesisMark 15:16-39 In One Movement ⌄

Mark 15:16-39 moves from mock coronation to crucifixion, from public insult to cosmic darkness, from Jesus' cry of abandonment to his death, and from the torn temple curtain to the centurion's confession. Everyone mocks the idea that Jesus is king because he will not save himself. But Mark shows that this is exactly how he reigns. He stays on the cross, enters shame, bears abandonment, dies, and opens the way to God. The crucified Jesus is not exposed as false; he is revealed as the Son of God.

FormationThe Shape of a Kingdom People ⌄

A kingdom people worship the King who did not come down. The mockers thought escape would prove Jesus' identity. The gospel says his love is proven because he stayed. We do not worship a self-protecting king, but the crucified Son who gave himself for us.

A kingdom people do not sanitize suffering. Mark makes us look at mockery, thorns, spitting, nails, darkness, and death. The cross speaks hope to suffering people because God has entered suffering in the body of Jesus, not because suffering is unreal.

A kingdom people live through the opened curtain. The way to God has been opened from top to bottom. We do not approach God by proving ourselves worthy. We come through the crucified Son whose death has opened access for the unworthy.

Gospel FluencyGospel Shaped Reflection ⌄

From this passage we learn that the heart of the gospel is not Jesus showing power by escaping the cross, but Jesus showing love by staying there. He is mocked as king, but he reigns by giving himself. He is told to save himself, but he saves others by refusing to come down. He dies in darkness, and the curtain tears. We come to God through the crucified Son whose death opens the way.

Who is God?
1

Who is God?

  • God reveals his kingship through the crucified Jesus, not through domination or self-protection (vv. 16-20, 26).
  • God is present in the place of public shame, bodily suffering, and violent death (vv. 21-27).
  • God saves through Jesus' refusal to save himself (vv. 29-32).
  • God hears lament from the deepest place of forsakenness (v. 34).
  • God opens access to his presence through the death of his Son (v. 38).
  • God reveals Jesus as Son of God at the cross (v. 39).
What has God done?
2

What has God done?

  • Jesus has endured mock coronation, violence, spitting, and public humiliation (vv. 16-20).
  • Jesus has been crucified under the true-but-mocking title, "the king of the Jews" (vv. 24-26, NIV).
  • Jesus has stayed on the cross while others demanded that he save himself (vv. 29-32).
  • Jesus has entered darkness, lament, abandonment, and real death (vv. 33-37).
  • God has torn the temple curtain from top to bottom, opening the way into his presence (v. 38).
  • God has drawn confession from a Gentile centurion at the foot of the cross (v. 39).
Who are we in light of what God has done?
3

Who are we in light of what God has done?

  • We are people who do not have to prove ourselves by coming down from weakness or shame.
  • We are sinners saved by the King who did not save himself.
  • We are invited near to God through the torn curtain, not through our worthiness.
  • We are people whose suffering can be brought to the crucified Son without pretending it does not hurt.
  • We are called to confess Jesus as Son of God precisely at the cross.
  • We are formed by self-giving love rather than self-protective power.
What wrong behavior, harmful emotions, or false attitudes result when we forget these truths?
4

What wrong behavior, harmful emotions, or false attitudes result when we forget these truths?

  • We mock weakness and assume God must prove himself through visible escape or control.
  • We demand signs before trust and spectacle before worship.
  • We try to save ourselves through image, achievement, strength, or avoidance of shame.
  • We sanitize the pain of others because we have sanitized the cross.
  • We live as if access to God still depends on our performance.
  • We treat lament as faithlessness instead of bringing abandonment honestly before God.
  • We seek forms of power that contradict the crucified King.
How would we live if we believed these truths?
5

How would we live if we believed these truths?

  • We would worship Jesus for staying on the cross instead of demanding that he prove himself by coming down.
  • We would come near to God with confidence because the curtain has been torn by grace.
  • We would bring our lament honestly to God, knowing Jesus has prayed from the place of forsakenness.
  • We would stand with suffering people without offering clean answers too quickly.
  • We would stop measuring God's love by whether he removes every cross immediately.
  • We would confess Jesus publicly as Son of God in a world that still mocks crucified love.
  • We would become less self-protective and more self-giving because this is the shape of our King.
If we lived this out, how would it impact our neighborhood, family, church, workplace, and friends?
6

If we lived this out, how would it impact our neighborhood, family, church, workplace, and friends?

  • Our homes would become places where suffering and lament can be spoken without shame.
  • Our churches would become communities that draw near to God through grace rather than performance.
  • Our worship would become more cross-shaped, less obsessed with visible strength, success, or control.
  • Our friendships would become safer for people who feel abandoned, mocked, or crushed.
  • Our workplaces would see people less driven by self-protection and more willing to bear costly love.
  • Our neighborhoods would encounter Christians who stand near the suffering because our Lord was revealed on a cross.
Prayerful LandingHearing the Father's Voice ⌄

The Bible is God's love letter to us. When we read it, we do not want accusation, fear, shame, or distance to distort what God has spoken. In this passage, the Father shows us his Son mocked, crucified, forsaken, and dead, and then shows us the curtain torn open and the Son confessed at the cross. The words that follow are written to help us hear the Father's love through the passage we have just studied.

My child, do not look away from my Son on the cross. He did not come down because his love was holding him there for you. When you feel mocked, ashamed, abandoned, or far from me, look at him. He has entered the darkness, and he has opened the way home.

>

The curtain is torn. You do not have to claw your way into my presence or prove that you are worthy to be near. In my crucified Son, I have come near to you. Rest in his finished love, bring me your lament, and let his cross make you tender, brave, and free.
